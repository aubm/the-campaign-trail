## A Meeting at the Manor

*Two months earlier · Zarraï’s study*

The gnome had laid his gloves in his lap. Since arriving, he had touched neither the tea nor the contract.

Zarraï waited on the other side of the desk. The window behind him overlooked the garden, where a servant was gathering branches that had fallen during the night.

“It’s an adjustment,” Veller went on.

He pushed the document forward a few inches.

“A reduction of a third,” Zarraï said.

“For all three warehouses. You keep a reliable tenant; we keep our business running. Under the present circumstances, that deserves consideration.”

Zarraï opened the contract at the page marked with a ribbon.

“You’re also asking for the right to terminate the lease without compensation.”

“A precaution.”

“And to pay the rent at the end of each quarter.”

“The company’s cash flow…”

“I know that part. You put it in writing.”

“You’re preparing for a voyage, as I understand it. We would have preferred to spare you the trouble of leaving with this matter unresolved.”

Veller adjusted his gloves without putting them on.

A door closed in the corridor. Zarraï glanced towards it. His visitor followed his gaze, then examined the shelves and the portrait above the fireplace. He had been received in this room before. This time, he seemed to be taking stock of it.

“Your family has always placed its trust in us,” he said.

“My family has been renting you warehouses for eight years.”

“Precisely. We wish to preserve that relationship. But several of our associates are concerned.”

Zarraï closed the contract.

“About what?”

Veller took his time choosing his words.

“Since Elturel, certain sensitivities have… heightened. You must have noticed the reactions in the city yourself.”

He paused. His eyes had strayed to Zarraï’s horns before returning to the desk.

“We have goods to sell,” he continued. “Buyers to reassure. Your family’s name appears on the buildings, on the receipts…”

“On your contracts too.”

“That is precisely what we need to be able to discuss.”

Zarraï drew a leather folder towards him. He took out a letter and laid it between them.

Veller recognised his own seal.

“Your request for more space,” Zarraï said. “You also wished to rent the neighbouring building.”

“The situation has changed.”

“This letter is twelve days old.”

“Some of our partners had yet to express their reservations.”

“Which ones?”

“You’ll understand that I can’t…”

“Very well.”

Zarraï took the letter back and put it away.

“Then we won’t be extending your lease to cover another building. For the three warehouses you currently rent, these are the terms of renewal.”

He presented a second document.

Veller read through it. His thumb stopped moving along the seam of his glove.

“They’re the same.”

“Yes.”

“You haven’t heard what I’ve just explained.”

“You want more space at the harbour. You’re also afraid your associates will discover the owner’s name. I heard both.”

Veller leaned back in his chair.

“You’ll struggle to find a company willing to take these premises at the moment.”

Zarraï let the silence linger.

“The lease expires in six days. If you don’t wish to renew it, I’ll arrange the final inspection.”

“Our goods are still inside.”

“I know.”

“Two ships are due before the end of the week.”

“I know that too.”

At last, the visitor looked at his cup. The tea had stopped steaming.

Zarraï picked up the quill and set it beside the contract, without offering it to him.

“You may keep all three buildings on the usual terms. The first payment will be made tomorrow. If the name on the receipts troubles you, your company can find another address.”

Veller read the first page, then the second. He went back over a clause he already knew. Zarraï let him.

When he signed, the quill caught on the paper halfway through his name.

“I’m sorry you’re taking a business discussion so badly.”

Zarraï checked the signature.

“I’ll have your copy sent over this afternoon. During my voyage, you’ll send the payments to my steward. On the agreed dates.”

“Will you be away long?”

“I don’t yet know.”

He stood. The meeting was over.

Veller stopped at the door.

“Your father would at least have agreed to negotiate.”

Zarraï’s hand tightened on the back of his chair.

“You had eight years without a rent increase. He negotiated far better than I do.”

He waited for his visitor to leave, then closed the door.

Silence returned to the study. Outside, the servant had almost filled his wheelbarrow.

Zarraï checked the contract a second time, recorded the payment due and closed the ledger. His sleeve had ridden up a little. He pulled it down to his wrist.

There was a soft knock.

“Come in.”

His little brother poked his head through the partly open door.

“Has he gone?”

“Yes.”

“You said you’d come afterwards.”

Zarraï looked at the letters still waiting on the desk.

“I’m coming.”

He put down the quill before the child needed to ask a second time.

## The Little Thief

*The day of departure · aboard the Wisdom of the Seas*

Jana heard the shouting before she found the ship again.

She had bought bread, a waterskin and a needle a merchant had assured her was essential at sea. None of it fitted comfortably in her bag. She stopped to push the loaf beneath the flap, then looked up at the deck.

Some twenty men had gathered at the stern. A few had climbed onto crates to see. One sailor still had a coil of rope over his shoulder.

“Get back!”

The voice was young, shrill with anger.

Jana looked for Bran on the quay. She could not see him.

“I’m warning you, don’t take another step!”

She went up the gangplank.

At the stern, something struck the wood with a sharp crack. Two men stepped aside, swearing. Jana slipped into the space they had left.

A young half-elf was backed against the rail. Her brown hair had come loose from its tie and clung to her temples. She held a staff in both hands before her. Her cheeks were scarlet; she was catching her breath in quick, shallow gasps.

Facing her, a half-orc with rolled-up sleeves held a short club.

“Put that down,” the half-orc ordered.

“So you can do it again?”

“You’re aboard a ship. When the first mate gives an order, you obey it.”

“He’s a child! You’d have smashed his skull!”

Her voice broke on the last word. She struck the deck with the end of her staff, then raised it so quickly that the half-orc stepped back.

“And I intend to finish the job, if you’ll move aside.”

“He took something to eat! That’s all he did!”

“From our provisions.”

“He was hungry!”

“They’re always hungry.”

Adwayria’s eyes widened. She was almost choking with indignation.

“Can you hear yourself? I spoke to him! He was looking for food for his family!”

A sailor smothered a laugh in his sleeve. His neighbour turned away, lips pressed tight; a small sound escaped him all the same.

Adwayria’s head snapped round.

“You think this is funny?”

The tip of her staff followed the movement. The man with the rope ducked and bumped into his neighbour.

“Watch what you’re doing with that!”

“Then tell them to get back!”

She felt behind her without taking her eyes off the crew, one arm stretched across a gap between the sacks. Jana could see only an open crate and the lower edge of a canvas sheet stirring against the deck.

The first mate reached for the staff.

“Don’t touch me!”

Jana stepped forward too.

“Wait.”

The first mate turned towards her.

“Another one?”

“Is anyone hurt?” Jana asked.

“Not yet!” Adwayria snapped.

She drew the staff back against her, then pointed it at the club again.

Jana looked at the weapon. The day before, she had seen a boot come down on a porter’s hand. Her own wrist still hurt.

“Could you lower your weapon?”

The first mate jerked her chin towards the staff.

“Start with her.”

“Just let him go!” Adwayria protested. “I’ll take him back to his family.”

“His family needs dealing with too,” the first mate retorted.

“Just you try!”

This time, the first mate raised her free hand.

“No one’s going anywhere. We’re waiting for the captain.”

“Let him come! You can explain it to him!”

“What did he take?” Jana asked.

“Grain!” the first mate replied.

“To feed his family!” Adwayria insisted.

“Doran?”

Bran’s voice turned several heads.

The circle opened before him. The captain looked at his first mate, the staff pointed at her, then the men perched on the crates. He raised an eyebrow.

“Did you need everyone for this?”

The first mate opened her mouth. Adwayria spoke first.

“She wanted to kill him!”

Bran gave Jana a brief nod, then approached the half-elf. He kept his hands in plain sight.

“Easy, lass. I’ve come to listen.”

“Then tell her to put that away!”

“I’ll see to it. Who did she want to kill?”

The first mate pointed to the sacks.

“That one.”

A small grey head appeared at the edge of the canvas.

The rat froze, whiskers twitching.

Jana looked at him.

Then she looked at the club.

Then the rat.

Bran pressed his lips together. He ran a hand over his beard, not quite managing to hide the smile that had escaped him.

“He’s frightened!” Adwayria cried.

“Yes,” Bran replied. “With all these people around him, I can understand why.”

“You can see how small he is!”

“Leave him in the hold for a week and see,” the first mate put in.

Laughter rippled through the crew.

“There’s nothing funny about it!” Adwayria protested.

She had almost shouted. Bran raised a hand towards the sailors, and the laughter subsided.

“I found him in a torn sack,” the first mate went on. “She got between us. I tried to move her aside; she hit my fingers.”

“You hit my fingers with your club!” Adwayria protested.

“And you hit mine,” Doran retorted. “Put that staff down before we start again.”

Bran looked at his first mate’s fingers, then at the staff Adwayria still gripped.

“I see the negotiations have been difficult.”

He laid a hand on Doran’s shoulder and gave it a gentle pat.

“Let it go, Doran.”

“Bran, those creatures gnaw through sacks, eat our stores and spread disease.”

“You’re right. She’ll put him ashore. We’ll be rid of him, and you can get back to loading.”

Doran looked at the rat, then at Adwayria. She snorted and tucked the club into her belt.

Bran turned back to Adwayria, a smile still tugging at his lips.

“There. She won’t hurt him. You have my word.”

Adwayria checked that the club was safely tucked away.

“Your turn now. Lower that staff. My men have seen enough of it for one day.”

She lowered it slowly.

“I’ll take him back to the others.”

“Very good. You can take all the time you need on the quay.”

He straightened and looked around at the crew.

“Come on, back to your posts. Let’s finish loading.”

The sailors dispersed. Doran herself set one man moving with a tap on the arm, then pointed to the crates still on the quay.

Adwayria stood still for a moment, her mouth half open, as though she had much more to say. She took a deep breath, pushed the hair from her face and knelt before the sacks.

She gently drew back the canvas and murmured a few words Jana did not understand. A moment later, the rat was climbing into her cupped hand.

## The Family on the Quay

*Moments later · Grey Harbour*

Jana followed her to the gangplank.

“Would you like me to carry your staff?”

Adwayria hesitated, then handed it to her.

“Thank you.”

She went down with the rat held against her tunic. On the quay, she searched for a suitable spot for a long time before crouching beside a pile of timber, away from wheels and feet.

The animal sniffed her fingers. He jumped to the ground, vanished beneath a plank, then emerged on the other side.

Adwayria watched him until he reached the wall of a warehouse.

“There. He’ll find the others.”

Jana handed back her staff.

“Did he really tell you about his family?”

“There are twenty-two brothers and sisters. And they’ve been finding less food since the sacks were moved.”

She pointed to the goods still being loaded.

Jana followed her gaze.

“You should have started by explaining that.”

“That’s what I did.”

There was no irony in Adwayria’s reply.

Jana felt a smile coming. She held it back when she saw the red marks on the girl’s fingers.

“Did you hurt yourself?”

“It’s all right.”

“Let me see.”

Still a little wary, Adwayria held out her hand. Jana checked the joints. The fingers moved normally.

“Nothing broken. You can run some cool water over them.”

“Are you a healer?”

“I trained at the temple of Lathander.”

“I’m Adwayria.”

“I’d heard.”

This time, they both smiled.

“Jana Coursevent.”

“Are you coming with us?”

“Yes.”

Adwayria gazed at the ship.

“I hope voyages don’t all begin like this.”

Jana shifted her bag back onto her shoulder.

“This is my first.”

## Introductions

*Before noon · on deck*

Bran was waiting for them by the mainmast. A tiefling stood beside him, a piece of luggage at his feet.

His dark clothes were carefully tailored. Despite the heat, his sleeves reached his wrists. What struck Jana most was how still he stood among the men passing with their loads.

“I see our guest is ashore,” Bran said.

“He’s gone back to the warehouses,” Adwayria replied.

The captain acknowledged this detail with a nod.

“Jana. Adwayria. This is Zarraï, who is also travelling to Port Nyanzaru.”

The tiefling inclined his head a little.

“We’ve finished loading your belongings,” Bran told him. “I’ve had the lashings checked.”

“Thank you,” Zarraï replied.

“If your family entrusts me with anything else, I want to be able to deliver it in good condition.”

“They’ll appreciate that.”

Adwayria looked from one man to the other.

“Have you known each other long?”

“I’ve done business with his family,” Bran replied.

“So you’ve been to Chult before?” she asked Zarraï.

“No.”

“Neither have I. Syndra wrote to ask me to come. Do you know Syndra?”

“By name,” Zarraï replied.

Bran ducked to avoid a yard being moved behind him.

“We’ll have time to talk about all that. For now, I’m going to get this ship under way before another creature asks to go ashore.”

Adwayria opened her mouth, but a sailor was already calling to the captain from the bow.

Bran left them together.

For a while, they watched the men at work.

“I didn’t see you earlier,” Adwayria said to Zarraï.

“I was below.”

“Did you hear?”

“Yes.”

She waited. He added nothing.

“The first mate wanted to kill a rat,” she explained.

“I’d gathered that part.”

Jana lowered her head to tighten a strap on her bag.

Adwayria studied the tiefling’s face. She seemed to be trying to work out whether he was making fun of her. His expression gave her no answer.

“He’s ashore now,” she concluded.

“Good,” Zarraï replied.

A sailor asked them to clear the way. Zarraï picked up his luggage and accompanied them to the rail.

## The Gangplank

*Noon · the Chionthar*

The first mooring line fell into the water with a soft splash.

Adwayria leaned over to watch it.

“Are we leaving?”

“As soon as they take away the gangplank,” Jana replied.

Two men lifted it almost at once.

Adwayria straightened.

Her gaze moved from the gap that had just opened between the quay and the hull to the last porters, then to the streets behind them.

“I thought we’d still have…”

She did not finish.

Jana set her bag at her feet.

“Have you forgotten something?”

Adwayria shook her head.

The wood creaked beneath them. Orders travelled from one end of the deck to the other. Above them, a sail unfurled with a crack.

Jana looked at the masts. She had paid for her passage, bought her waterskin, found her berth. Now the city was really beginning to draw away.

She thought of the road from Beregost, of her horse left at the stables, then of the blanket across Celeborn’s knees.

Beside her, Adwayria had fallen silent.

Jana stayed beside her without trying to find something to ask.

A little farther along, Zarraï had rested a hand on the rail. He was looking beyond the warehouses, towards the rooftops and the higher parts of the city. When a sailor passed behind him, he stepped aside, then returned to his place.

Bran raised his arm.

“Off we go!”

Beside the warehouse wall, the rat stopped nibbling.

A rope had just scraped across the stones. He backed beneath a plank, the bit of grain between his paws.

Through the opening, he could still see the great hull. Above it, the girl was leaning towards the quay. She raised a hand.

The rat twitched his whiskers.

The hull slipped slowly past his hiding place. The girl drifted away with it, beside another pale figure and a man with dark horns, turned towards the city.

Soon, he could see only the stern.

The rat waited for the footsteps to fade. He picked up his grain and slipped between the planks, towards the small squeaks waiting for him in the darkness.
