The Gates at Dusk

Dusk · the Outer City, then the Lower City

The horse stopped before she asked him to.

Jana stayed bent over his neck for a moment. She had pushed the pace for three days; now she had to pull on the reins to coax him a few steps farther. She dismounted. Her knees promptly reminded her that she had scarcely left the saddle.

A strand had escaped her long black braid and stuck to her cheek. Dust dulled her pale armour with its golden trim; a rising sun stood out on the breastplate. Weariness hollowed her young face, but her brown eyes still followed the movements of the crowd.

Ahead, travellers squeezed between the Outer City’s stalls and the open gate in the walls. A man pushed a wheelbarrow. Another cursed over a split bundle. Above their heads, Jana could make out houses climbing towards still higher walls.

She joined the queue with her horse.

“The animal stays outside,” a guard said.

She looked up.

“I’m looking for an inn.”

The guard pointed to the stables behind her.

“So is he, by the look of it.”

The horse had lowered his head almost to the cobbles.

Jana retraced her steps.

At the stables, she had him unsaddled, checked the water and paid for several days’ care. The boy taking the reins asked whether she would be leaving soon.

She had no answer.

As she unfastened her belongings, she saw again the blanket across Celeborn’s knees. She did not know why that detail kept returning: a corner of wool carefully folded over fingers that could no longer hold hers.

He had given her a name. Artus Cimber.

She had to start somewhere.

Jana rested her forehead against the horse’s neck.

“Forgive me. Get some rest.”

By the time she finally passed through the gate, the last merchants were packing away their stalls. She paid the toll, then followed a street downhill between closely packed buildings. A green lantern lit up above a shop. Farther on, another cast bottle-green light into the mist.

She was looking for a sign promising food when a man fell a few paces ahead of her.

Two others emerged from the passage beside a shop.

“You’re not done paying,” one of them called.

The Alley

Evening · a passage in the Lower City

The man on the ground clutched a small purse to his chest. His clothes bore the dust of sacks carried on his back.

“I paid this morning.”

The broader of the two seized his collar.

“Let him get up,” Jana said.

They looked at her. The one standing farther back smiled at the sight of her travelling gear.

“Keep walking.”

She set her bag against the wall, watching their hands.

“He says he’s paid.”

“You keeping the books now?” the second man retorted.

The porter tried to crawl towards the street. A boot came down on his hand.

Jana stepped forward. She did not think about the three days on the road, or the soup she had almost been able to smell a moment earlier. She saw the fingers crushed beneath the sole and shoved the man’s shoulder.

He staggered back a step. Far enough for the porter to free his hand.

Not far enough to fall.

The blow came from so close that she never saw it coming. Her cheek struck the wall; white light flashed across her vision.

She heard both men laughing.

Jana pushed herself upright against the stone.

“Get out of here. Both of you.”

The broader man reached for his belt.

The knife looked almost small, until it moved.

Jana held out her palm.

“Lathander, guide my hand.”

A glow descended towards the man. He threw himself aside; the radiance faded on the cobbles without touching him.

He looked at the spot where he had stood a second before.

Then he stopped smiling.

“Grab her,” the man with the knife ordered his companion.

The other man circled the crates.

Jana backed towards the street. She could still run. But the porter remained on the ground, between her and the blade.

“Come towards me,” Jana told him.

He tried to get up.

The man with the knife caught his sleeve. The fabric pulled taut, the blade slipped, and the porter cried out.

Blood ran over his hand.

Jana took a step towards him. The second man blocked her way.

She raised her palm again; he caught her wrist and slammed her against the crates.

Wood cracked behind her back.

“Try that again,” the second man taunted.

His grip was tight enough to numb her fingers.

Jana pulled at her arm. He pulled harder. She let herself be dragged forward, drove her shoulder into his chest and felt his grip loosen.

She wrenched her wrist free.

The porter had dragged himself behind them. The man with the knife was about to catch him.

Jana reached out, this time past the shoulder of the man still holding her by her clothes.

The light fell.

It struck the armed man’s upper arm. He cried out, his knees buckled, and he caught himself against the wall. The knife stayed in his hand.

Jana did not wait.

She caught the porter under the arm and hauled him towards the street. He was heavier than she had expected; his heels scraped the stones.

A hand closed around her bag.

She left it.

“Help!”

A shutter banged open.

“What are you doing down there?” a woman shouted from the window.

“Open your door!” Jana called back.

The woman vanished from the window.

Behind Jana, the knife scraped the wall. The wounded man was back on his feet.

The door stayed shut.

She backed farther away, holding the porter against her. He was slipping. She could not keep him upright much longer.

“You! Go and fetch the Flaming Fist!” called a voice from the street.

A deep voice, used to making itself heard.

The second attacker turned his head. Several passers-by had stopped at the entrance to the passage.

At last, a bolt grated.

“Here!” the woman called.

Jana pushed the porter inside. The man with the knife kept coming. He held his arm close to his body, but his anger had not diminished.

She stayed on the threshold, her hand raised.

He looked at the witnesses behind her, then at his companion.

The other man had already dropped the bag.

“That’s enough. We’re leaving.”

They backed down the passage, without running at first. Jana did not lower her hand until she saw them turn the corner at the far end.

Only then did she realise she had been holding her breath.

Trembling Hands

Moments later · inside the shop

Inside the shop, the porter breathed in short, uneven gasps. Jana crouched in front of him.

“Show me your arm.”

He was watching the door.

“They’re gone. Look at me,” Jana said again.

The shopkeeper brought a basin. Jana asked her for a clean cloth, then had the wounded man move his fingers. They responded.

She held her palm above the cut.

Her first breath trembled. She tried again, more slowly.

A gentle warmth passed between her fingers. The bleeding eased, then stopped; the wound closed, leaving a pink line.

The porter moved his hand.

“How much do I owe you?”

“Nothing.”

She cleaned the blood left on his skin.

The shopkeeper held out a second cloth.

“For your face.”

Jana pressed it to her cheek. She had forgotten.

Outside, an old dwarf picked up her bag and set it by the door.

“All there, I think.”

She recognised the voice that had called to the passers-by.

“Thank you,” Jana told him.

He looked at the wounded man, then at the shopkeeper.

“Do you still need a hand?”

“My boy will see him home,” the shopkeeper replied.

The dwarf nodded and walked away.

Jana stayed until the porter could stand. He asked where she was going.

“To eat. And to find someone.”

He directed her to the Elfsong Tavern, near the Basilisk Gate.

As she picked up her things, she looked at her fingers. They were still trembling.

Yet they had managed to heal this man.

At Celeborn’s bedside, she had watched them shine with the same light. Day after day. To no avail.

The Elfsong

Later that evening · an alcove in the common room

The warmth of the tavern made her realise how cold she was.

A large fireplace lit the tables. Alcoves offered quieter places to sit, and in one of them Jana spotted the dwarf from the alley, busy pushing a ledger away from his plate.

He beckoned to her.

“Did you get all your belongings back? I’m more used to cargo than personal luggage.”

“I haven’t checked yet.”

“Please do. I prefer complaints before dessert.”

She opened her bag. Nothing seemed to be missing.

The dwarf waited, then indicated the empty chair.

“Bran Silverbeard. Can I offer you something to drink?”

“Jana Coursevent. Water, thank you.”

She sat facing the room.

The meal arrived before she had decided how to begin the conversation. She briefly bowed her head over the bowl.

Bran asked her no questions during the first few spoonfuls.

“Did you see what happened?” Jana asked at last.

“The end of it. You needed witnesses. They don’t much like working in front of an audience.”

“Do you know them?”

“No. I know the city.”

He passed her the bread.

“The man you helped, though, I’ve seen him loading sacks on the docks.”

Jana wiped her fingers on the edge of the cloth.

“Are you a sailor?”

“A captain and a merchant. Depending on who’s asking for money, I sometimes prefer to be the other.”

She smiled.

“Do you hear many names on the docks?”

“A few. Which one are you looking for?”

“Artus Cimber.”

Bran’s knife stopped above the bread.

“Cimber? I met him in Port Nyanzaru.”

Jana looked up.

“When?”

“Over a year ago. I’d have to go through my accounts to be more precise.”

“Are you sure of the name?”

He repeated it.

She put down her spoon.

The dwarf offering her supper happened to know the very man she was looking for. She wanted to be glad. Something held her back.

“And now you’re going to offer to take me there.”

Bran looked at her for a moment, then set down his knife.

“Yes. My ship sails for Chult tomorrow. Put like that, I admit it sounds like an excellent reason to keep your purse shut.”

“So you understand why I’m asking.”

“Very well.”

“How did you meet him?”

Bran told her about a conversation on the waterfront during one of his visits. He had neither travelled nor done business with Cimber; their errands had simply kept them in the same place for part of the morning.

“Why would he have told you his name?” Jana asked.

“I’d told him mine. It’s still quite a common custom.”

He let his smile fade without trying to win one from her.

“Listen: I can show you my ship, my cargo and people who know me. For that conversation, all I have is my word. You’re right to distinguish between the two.”

Jana slowly picked up her spoon again.

“Celeborn sent me to find him. An old acquaintance of his. He thinks Artus could help him.”

“Your father?”

“My teacher.”

She looked at the bread between them.

“He raised me.”

Bran closed his ledger.

She told him about Beregost, the temple, Celeborn always finding time to answer her questions. About his death three years earlier, at the hands of the undead. About the relief when he was resurrected.

There had been so many tomorrows since then that she had stopped counting.

“Now he’s wasting away. People speak of a curse that affects those who’ve been brought back. I can heal a wound. This, I don’t know how to stop.”

Bran did not try to explain it to her.

“He was too weak to come with me,” Jana went on. “He only told me to find Cimber. He was so certain that…”

She broke off.

The light had dimmed.

Jana looked up at the lamps. A voice was singing, close enough for her to look for a woman among the tables. No one had stood up.

By the fire, a patron put down his dice without rolling them.

The voice seemed to come from behind Jana, then from above them. She turned.

“Who’s singing?”

Bran answered quietly.

“The one this place is named after.”

“You mean a spirit?”

She stiffened.

“So they say,” Bran replied. “She sings of a love lost at sea.”

Jana listened. She could see neither body nor face, only patrons who had fallen silent.

“Who’s keeping her here?”

“I don’t know.”

She waited for more of an explanation. None came.

The lament went on for a while. Jana kept her hands on the table. Everything she had been taught told her to be wary; yet all she could hear was grief.

When the voice fell silent, the lights brightened again. A chair scraped the floor.

Jana looked at Bran.

“What’s your ship called?”

“The Wisdom of the Seas.”

“Tell me where to find her.”

The Night Between

Night · an upstairs room

Jana went upstairs with a ship’s name, directions to the docks and the price of a passage.

Before leaving the common room, she had questioned the server. He knew Bran. His sailors ate here when they were in port; people had seen them loading since morning.

That proved nothing about Artus.

She sat on the bed and took off her shoes. The attacker’s fingers had left marks on her wrist.

Several weeks at sea, Bran had said.

She had been gone for three days, and already she felt she had waited too long.

Jana knelt beside the bed.

“Lathander, watch over him until I return.”

She stayed there, unable to find any more words.

When she lay down, she thought she would never sleep.

A footstep in the corridor made her open her eyes. Grey light already outlined the shutters.

She got up too quickly, winced, then stood again more carefully.

Before the Wisdom of the Seas

The next morning · Grey Harbour

On her way down to Grey Harbour, Jana finally glimpsed the river between the rooftops.

The Chionthar was wider than she had imagined. Mist still hid some of the ships; their masts emerged before their hulls. Behind her, the streets climbed towards the Upper City’s walls, where the sun was beginning to touch the stone.

On the quay, no one seemed to find the morning particularly beautiful.

Barrels were being counted. Someone was calling for a rope. A man was looking for someone who swore he was elsewhere.

Jana asked a porter for the Wisdom of the Seas, then a sailor beside the ship he had pointed out. Bran’s name made both men look in the same direction.

The captain was ashore, inspecting a load of cargo.

He waited for a crate to be set down before coming over to her.

“Did you sleep?”

“Yes.”

“One sensible decision already. I’ll try not to ask too many more of you before noon.”

She took in the hull, the rigging, the men at work. The ship existed. It really was going to sail.

“What if Artus is no longer in Port Nyanzaru?” Jana asked.

The dwarf’s face grew more serious.

“We’ll have to find someone who’s seen him since I did. I’ll introduce you to the people I know. But a year is a long time.”

“He could have left Chult.”

“Yes.”

She watched a rope being hauled onto the deck.

“I’d rather you told me otherwise.”

“It would make the sale easier.”

She looked at him. He said nothing more.

“What should I prepare for after that? If I have to leave the port?”

“A guide. Water. Something to keep your supplies dry. And a certain tolerance for insects.”

“And getting around?”

“On foot, or by canoe, depending on the route. Your guide will tell you.”

“Is it as hot as they say?”

“Yes. Expect to be soaked much of the time, without feeling much cooler.”

“Do you know the jungle?” Jana asked.

“From the ship, it’s magnificent. For a closer account, you’ll have to find someone else.”

“They say there are undead.”

“Take those stories seriously.”

She nodded. This time, he was no longer smiling.

“I won’t leave people to face those things alone.”

Bran glanced at her bruised wrist.

“I thought as much.”

A sailor called to him. He raised a finger, asking him to wait.

Jana took out her purse.

She had him confirm the price, the sleeping arrangements and the meals, then counted out the coins. He handed one back that she had mistaken for another.

She closed her fingers around it.

“I’ll be ready before noon.”

“Good. We’ll sail as soon as the loading is done.”

The sun was above the rooftops now. Jana felt its warmth on her aching cheek.

“Port Nyanzaru, then.”

“Port Nyanzaru.”
